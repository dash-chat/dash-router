Dash Router

Nearby devices store and forward each other's messages until they reach their recipients.

Why Dash Router

When the internet is not available, we need a way to send messages through a mesh of phones until they reach their recipients.

Dash Router is our prototype of that. So far we've built its first routing protocol. It only works on a LAN, with the phones connected to each other. In the future we'd like to add more protocols and transports.

It's integrated in Dash Chat, a messenger that works without internet and is built on p2panda.

The phones in between can't read your messages Dash Chat uses p2panda to encrypt messages with p2panda spaces before they leave your phone. A phone passing one along only ever holds encrypted data.

See it in Dash Chat

Try it yourself

Dash Router is in Dash Chat from version 0.23.1, but it's experimental and off by default. You need at least three devices on the same Wi-Fi or hotspot. On each one:

  1. Install Dash Chat from the download page.
  2. Go to Settings › Help and tap Version seven times. A "Developer mode enabled" message shows up.
  3. Back in Settings there is now a Developer options entry. Open it and turn on Enable Dash Router.

You need at least three devices to really see Dash Router in action, because Dash Chat already makes it easy for any two devices to connect peer-to-peer. Dash Router is only useful when there are two devices that can't connect directly (e.g. because they're not connected to the network at the same time) and need one or more intermediaries to relay messages between them.

How it works right now

Conversations are append-only logs, one per author, so a device can describe everything it has for a conversation as a few ranges of sequence numbers. The protocol's two messages are built on that:

Want A list of what the sender already has. Anyone who has more is being asked to send the difference. A Want doesn't say who it came from.
Have Actual messages, sent because somebody's Want showed they were missing.

Relaying is deliberately dumb. A device that receives a Want or a Have forwards it to everyone else straight away, so every one of them ends up crossing the whole network. What keeps that from getting out of hand is being careful about when a device decides to create a new Want or Have.

  1. You send a message. Your phone waits a moment and then sends it out as a Have. Most of the time that's all it takes.
  2. Devices ask for what they missed. Messages get lost, and phones join and leave the network over time. So every device also sends a Want now and then, on a random timer, saying what it has so far. Before it does, it looks at the Wants it has heard recently and leaves out anything that's already been asked for by others. If everything that a device wants has already been recently asked for by others, it doesn't send a Want at all. In practice, one device ends up asking on behalf of everybody who is missing the same thing.
  3. Someone who has it answers. A device that hears a Want and has some of what's missing doesn't reply immediately either. It starts another random timer, and when that runs out it sends whatever is still missing. If someone else's Have covered it in the meantime, it sends less, or nothing.
  4. Old messages make room. The relay store has a size limit. Once a device hits it, it throws away the oldest message bodies first and keeps their headers, which are small.

Why the timers are random

So that devices don't all answer the same Want at the same moment and fill the network with copies. The one that happens to fire first does the work, and most or all of the others see the answer go by and drop it from their own.

How long the timers should be depends on how many devices are around and how many messages get lost. We're still tuning that, and it's what the simulations below are for. DESIGN.md has the full protocol.

Simulations

These are recordings of the protocol running in a simulator, in order of difficulty. Hit play or step through with the arrow buttons. Clicking a node shows its state.

  1. 1. A line of five Five devices in a row, one of them writing, nothing lost. Good for following a single message from one end to the other.
  2. 2. A small network Twenty devices, four of them writing, 2% of messages lost.
  3. 3. A lossy mesh Fifty devices, six of them writing, and 15% of messages lost on the way.

What you're looking at

  • Circles are devices. A device gets greener the more of the written messages it has, and its border flashes bright green when it writes one.
  • Each device has up to three small pies around it. The amber one is the countdown to its next Want. The blue one is the countdown to its next Have, and is only there while the device has something to answer. The green one shows how much of everything written so far it holds.
  • A link turns amber while a Want is crossing it and blue when a Have is.
